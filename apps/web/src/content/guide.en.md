# Cat Care Guide

From deciding to get a cat and bringing them home to keeping them company in old age, here's what you need to know. Every cat is different, so for anything about health or medication, follow your vet's advice.

## 🐱 Choosing and adopting a cat

**Think it through first**

- Cats usually live **12–18 years**, and some pass 20. A cat means well over a decade of company and costs
- Ask yourself: is anyone at home allergic? Does your lease allow pets? Who looks after the cat when you travel?

**Adopt or buy**

- Consider **adopting** first. Shelters and rescuers have many healthy, friendly cats, and an adult's personality is already settled, so it's easier to tell whether you'll get along
- If you buy, choose a reputable breeder, **ask to see the parents and where the kittens live**, and avoid pet-shop or online "buy now, ship today" sellers
- Kittens should ideally stay with their mother until **about 12 weeks**, and never leave before 8 weeks. Leaving too early can weaken socialization and immunity

**Signs of a healthy cat**

- Alert and curious, bright eyes, **clean eyes and nose** without heavy discharge
- No dark wax or smell in the ears; a smooth coat without bald patches or heavy dandruff
- A clean rear end with no signs of diarrhea
- Ask for **vaccination and deworming records** and whether the cat has been tested for infectious diseases

**Know common inherited diseases**

- **Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM)** is common in Ragdolls, British and American Shorthairs and Maine Coons; ask whether the parents had heart screening
- Persians and Exotic Shorthairs are prone to **polycystic kidney disease (PKD)**, and flat faces often bring breathing and tear-duct problems
- The fold in **Scottish Folds** comes from a cartilage defect. Nearly all of them develop joint problems that cause lifelong pain, so buying Folds isn't recommended

> 💡 Not sure you can commit long term? Sign up as a **foster home** for a shelter and try living with a cat first.

## 🧺 Before your cat comes home

**Essentials**

- **Litter box and litter**: ideally about 1.5× your cat's body length. Try a small bag first (tofu, clay or mixed) to see what your cat likes
- **Food**: start with whatever the previous home or breeder fed, then switch gradually
- **Food and water bowls**: ceramic or stainless steel are easiest to keep clean. Shallow, wide bowls keep whiskers from brushing the sides. Keep water apart from food
- **Scratching posts**: at least one vertical and one horizontal; the vertical one should be tall and sturdy enough for a full stretch
- **Carrier**: for vet visits and moving; models that also open from the top are easiest
- **A bed or soft blanket**, **wand toys and small toys**, **a brush and nail clippers**

**Make your home safe**

- Secure **windows and balconies**. Falls from height are common and insect screens won't hold a cat; install sturdy netting
- Put away **cords, hair ties, yarn, dental floss, plastic bags and sewing thread**. Swallowed string-like objects are very dangerous and often need surgery
- Keep toilet lids and washer/dryer doors closed, and check inside before starting a machine
- Lock away cleaners, insecticides, medicines and essential oils. **Many essential oils (such as tea tree and eucalyptus) are toxic to cats**; avoid heavy diffusing
- Check your plants: **lilies are extremely toxic to cats**; even the pollen or vase water can be fatal

> ⚠️ Licking a little lily pollen (true lilies and daylilies) can cause acute kidney failure in cats. It's safest to keep lilies out of a cat home entirely.

## 🏠 The first week

- **Start with one quiet room** with a litter box, water, food and somewhere to hide (an open carrier or a cardboard box)
- Let your cat come out on their own terms. **Don't force cuddles or stare.** Sitting quietly in the room doing your own thing is great company
- Hiding and eating little for a day or two is normal. But if your cat **eats nothing at all for more than 24 hours**, call your vet
- With children at home, agree on rules: no chasing, no disturbing the cat while it eats or sleeps, and picking up only with an adult nearby
- If you have other cats, **keep the newcomer separate for 1–2 weeks** until a vet check is clear, then introduce them slowly as described in "Multi-cat homes"
- **See a vet within the first week** to check for parasites, ear mites and infectious disease, and plan vaccines, deworming and spaying/neutering

> 💡 A slow blink is how cats say they feel safe and friendly. Try slowly blinking at your cat first; they'll often blink back.

## 🍽️ Food and feeding

**Choosing food**

- Cats are **obligate carnivores** and need nutrients like taurine from meat. Pick food labelled "complete and balanced" with meat listed first
- Kitten food until about 12 months, then adult food; senior formulas are an option for older cats. Prescription diets only as your vet advises
- **Wet food helps a lot with hydration**. Many cats naturally drink little, so mixing wet and dry, or feeding mostly wet, keeps water intake up
- Treats, freeze-dried snacks and lickable treats are extras: **keep them under 10% of daily calories**

**How much and how**

- Start from the amount on the package and adjust for weight and body shape. Ideal: **you can feel the ribs but not see them, and there's a visible waist from above**
- Kittens can eat 3–4 meals a day; for adults, 2–3 measured meals keep weight in check better than an always-full bowl
- **Puzzle feeders and treat balls** turn meals into a game and slow down fast eaters
- Change food slowly, **over 7–10 days**, mixing in more of the new food each day. Sudden switches often cause diarrhea
- Homemade raw diets aren't recommended without expert help: they're hard to balance and carry bacteria and parasite risks. Talk to your vet or a pet nutritionist first

**Water**

- Refresh water and wash the bowl daily. Several bowls around the home, or a fountain, encourage drinking
- Keep water away from the litter box; some cats also prefer it not right next to their food

**Never feed your cat**

- Onion, garlic, leeks and chives (raw, cooked or powdered; they damage red blood cells)
- Chocolate, coffee, tea, energy drinks or anything with caffeine
- Grapes and raisins
- Alcohol, raw yeast dough
- Anything containing xylitol (gum, some sugar-free snacks)
- Cooked bones, including fish and chicken bones (they splinter)
- Milk: most adult cats are lactose intolerant. If you want to offer some, use lactose-free pet milk
- Raw eggs and raw fish (bacteria; a long-term raw-fish diet can also cause vitamin B1 deficiency)

> ⚠️ Never give your cat human medicine. Acetaminophen (paracetamol, found in many cold and fever remedies) is extremely dangerous to cats even in tiny doses, and painkillers like ibuprofen are dangerous too.

## 🚽 Litter boxes

- The rule of thumb is **one box per cat, plus one**: two cats, three boxes. In multi-storey homes, put at least one on each floor
- Put them somewhere **quiet and easy to reach**, away from food and water and from noisy appliances like washing machines
- Many cats prefer **large, uncovered boxes**. Covered boxes trap smells and some cats avoid them
- Fill litter **5–7 cm (2–3 in)** deep so they can dig and bury
- **Scoop at least once a day**, replace all the litter every 1–2 weeks and wash the box with warm water and a mild detergent. Avoid strongly scented cleaners and perfumed litter
- Watch what's in the box: clumps suddenly bigger and more frequent (drinking more), fewer and smaller, very loose or very hard stool, or blood all signal a health problem

> 💡 A cat that stops using the litter box isn't taking revenge. Usually the box isn't clean enough or is in the wrong place, or your cat is unwell (urinary problems, joint pain). Get a vet check first.

## 🩺 Vaccines, parasites and check-ups

**Vaccines**

- **Core vaccine** (feline panleukopenia, herpesvirus and calicivirus; often called FVRCP): kittens usually start at **8–9 weeks**, with a dose every 3–4 weeks until **16 weeks or older**, a booster a year later, then typically every 1–3 years
- **Rabies**: as required locally and advised by your vet
- Whether other vaccines (such as feline leukemia) are needed depends on whether your cat goes outdoors and lives with other cats; ask your vet
- Watch your cat for a day after each shot: mild tiredness or reduced appetite is common, but **facial swelling, vomiting or trouble breathing** need a vet right away

**Parasite control**

- **Internal (worms)**: typically every 3 months; kittens more often, as your vet advises
- **External (fleas, ticks)**: typically monthly. Indoor-only cats can still get fleas carried in on people
- Use **cat-specific** products dosed by weight. Dog flea treatments often contain ingredients (such as permethrin) that are toxic to cats

**Spaying / neutering**

- Usually recommended **around 5–6 months**; agree the timing with your vet
- It reduces yowling, spraying and escape attempts during heat and lowers the risk of uterine infection and mammary tumors
- After surgery, use a cone or recovery suit so they don't lick the wound. **Neutered cats gain weight more easily**, so reduce portions accordingly

**Regular check-ups**

- Adults: **once a year**. Seniors: **every six months**, including weight, teeth and heart checks, with blood and urine tests recommended for older cats
- Weigh your cat monthly at home and keep a record. Weight changes often reveal health problems before anything else does

## ✨ Everyday care

- **Brushing**: short-haired cats 1–2 times a week, long-haired cats ideally daily, paying attention to armpits, belly and behind the ears where knots form. Less shedding, fewer hairballs
- **Nail trims**: every 2–3 weeks. Only trim the clear tip; **avoid the pink quick**. It's fine to do a few nails at a time over several days
- **Teeth**: use a cat toothbrush and cat toothpaste, **never human toothpaste**. Start by letting them lick the paste and build up slowly. Tartar and gum disease are common in adult cats
- **Baths**: most cats keep themselves clean and rarely need one. Bathe only when very dirty or advised by a vet, use a cat shampoo, and dry them fully
- **Eyes and ears**: wipe away eye discharge with a damp cotton pad; clean only the visible part of the ear and **never push cotton buds inside**. Lots of dark ear discharge may mean ear mites; see a vet
- **Body check**: while brushing, feel for new lumps, wounds or sore spots

## 🐾 Behavior, play and body language

**Play every day**

- At least **twice a day for 10–15 minutes**. Use a wand toy to mimic the hunt (stalk, chase, pounce, catch), let them "catch" the prey at the end, then give a little food. They'll be more satisfied and sleep better
- **Don't play with your bare hands**; cats learn hands are prey and bite more as adults
- Laser pointers are fine, but end on a real toy they can pounce on, or they'll feel frustrated by never catching anything

**Reading body language**

- **Tail straight up, tip curved**: happy, saying hello
- **Tail lashing or thumping**: irritated and about to get angry; stop what you're doing
- **Puffed-up tail, arched back, side-on stance**: frightened, trying to look bigger
- **Ears flat back, pupils wide, body low**: scared or ready to defend
- **Belly up**: a sign of trust, not necessarily an invitation to rub it
- **Head bumps and leg rubs**: marking you with their scent, a sign of affection
- **Kneading**: relaxed and content, a habit from nursing as a kitten
- **Purring**: usually relaxed and content, but cats also purr when in pain or stressed

**Common behavior problems**

- **Scratching furniture**: put a scratching post right next to the spot and reward them for using it. Don't declaw: it amputates the last toe bone and causes long-term pain
- **Night-time zoomies and wake-up calls**: have a good play session before bed, then a meal — hunt, eat, sleep
- **Biting and ankle attacks**: usually pent-up energy; add more playtime. When bitten, stop interacting and walk away; never shout or hit

> 💡 Punishment and shouting only make a cat afraid of you. Offer an alternative and reward them when they get it right.

## 🏡 Multi-cat homes

- **Plenty of resources, spread out**: bowls, water, litter boxes, scratchers and resting spots, each "one per cat plus one", in different places so no cat can guard them
- Add **vertical space** (cat trees, cupboard tops, shelves) so cats can share a room at different heights and avoid face-offs
- **Introduce a new cat slowly**; it can take weeks:
  - Start in separate rooms and **swap blankets and beds** so they learn each other's scent
  - Feed them on either side of a closed door, moving the bowls closer over time
  - Let them see each other through a pet gate; once both are relaxed, try short supervised meetings with play and treats as distractions
  - Hissing or growling means go back a step. Don't let them "fight it out"
- **Play or fight?** Play is quiet, with turn-taking and normal fur; real fights involve yowling, puffed fur and one cat fleeing or hiding for a long time

## 🧠 Enrichment and training

**A more interesting home**

- Indoor cats still need to hunt, climb, watch and hide. Provide **cat trees, a window perch, boxes and tunnels**
- **Rotate toys**: put out a few at a time and swap them every few days so they stay new
- Grow **cat grass** (wheat or oat grass) for chewing. About half to two-thirds of cats react to catnip; offer it now and then
- Hide treats in puzzle toys or scatter some kibble around the home so they can "forage"

**Cats can be trained**

- Cats respond well to **positive reinforcement**: reward the right behavior immediately with a tiny favorite treat
- **Clicker training** can teach coming when called, sitting, high-fives, even walking into the carrier
- The most useful things to train are **vet-related**: leave the carrier out as a bed with treats inside; handle paws, ears and mouth daily so nail trims and exams aren't scary
- Keep sessions to **3–5 minutes** and stop while they're still interested

## 🌡️ Seasons, travel and holidays

**Summer: prevent heatstroke**

- Keep the home ventilated or air-conditioned, with shade and plenty of water. **Never leave a cat alone in a car**, even for a few minutes
- Signs of heatstroke: **open-mouth panting, drooling, wobbling, bright red gums**. Move them somewhere cool, wipe them with a room-temperature damp towel (not ice water) and go to a vet immediately
- Brush long-haired cats more often; shaving them bare isn't recommended, as fur also insulates against heat

**Winter: warmth and safety**

- Provide a warm bed. Guard against burns and chewed cords with heating pads and heaters
- Before starting a car parked outside, knock on the hood: outdoor cats often hide in engines for warmth

**Travel, boarding and moving**

- Leave the carrier out for a few days beforehand; covering it with a blanket on the trip helps them feel safe
- Ask your vet before long journeys. **Don't give sedatives or motion-sickness pills on your own**
- When you go away, **a pet sitter at home** is usually less stressful than boarding; if you board, visit first and pack a familiar blanket
- When moving, keep your cat in one settled room until the movers are done and windows and doors are secure, then let them explore

**Holidays**

- During fireworks, close windows and doors and give them a quiet hiding spot; don't drag them out to "comfort" them
- Keep **ribbons, tinsel and garlands**, chocolate and lily bouquets out of reach

## 🤒 Common health problems

Cats are good at hiding illness. Talk to your vet if you notice:

**Urinary problems**

- Frequent trips to the box with little urine, blood in the urine, peeing outside the box, crying while peeing
- Stress, low water intake and obesity raise the risk. **A male cat who can't pee is an emergency** (see "Emergencies")

**Vomiting and hairballs**

- An occasional hairball is common, especially in long-haired cats. **Several times a week**, refusing food afterwards, lethargy, or retching without bringing anything up all need a vet
- Regular brushing and some dietary fiber help reduce hairballs

**Mouth problems**

- **Bad breath, drooling, dropping food, chewing on one side, pawing at the mouth** can mean tartar, gum disease, tooth resorption or stomatitis
- Dental disease is very common in adult cats; ask your vet to check the teeth at every visit

**Skin problems**

- Constant scratching, licking until bald, tiny black specks on the skin (possibly flea dirt)
- Round patches of hair loss with flaking may be **ringworm (a fungal infection)**, which can spread to people; see a vet and clean the home

**Upper respiratory infections**

- Sneezing, runny nose, eye discharge, red swollen eyes, sometimes with fever and poor appetite
- Common in kittens and newly adopted cats. A blocked nose means they can't smell food and may stop eating, so see a vet early

**Obesity**

- Obesity raises the risk of diabetes, arthritis, fatty liver disease and urinary problems
- Lose weight **slowly under vet guidance** (usually no more than 1–2% of body weight per week). **Never make a cat fast suddenly**

**Chronic diseases in older cats**

- **Chronic kidney disease**: drinking and peeing more, weight loss, a poor coat, occasional vomiting
- **Diabetes**: drinking, peeing and eating more yet losing weight; some cats walk on their hocks
- **Hyperthyroidism**: eating a lot but getting thinner, unusually active or vocal, fast heartbeat
- Caught early and managed well, cats can live comfortably for years with these, which is why regular blood tests for seniors matter

**Signs a cat is in pain**

- No longer jumping up, hiding, avoiding touch in one spot
- Grooming less (greasy, messy coat) or licking one area constantly
- Facial changes: squinting eyes, whiskers pulled forward, ears turned out to the sides
- Toileting outside the box, becoming grumpy

## 📅 Life stages

**Kittens (0–12 months)**

- Full of energy and in the key socialization window: introduce different people, sounds and places, plus everyday routines like toothbrushing, nail trims and the carrier
- Keep up with vaccines and deworming; feed kitten food in several meals a day

**Adults (1–7 years)**

- Regular meals and exercise; keep weight in check
- Yearly check-ups, parasite control and boosters; look after their teeth

**Seniors (7+, with 11+ considered geriatric)**

- Switch to a senior formula and weigh them monthly
- Check-ups with blood and urine tests every six months; watch for **drinking and peeing more**
- Joints may stiffen: add steps up to favorite spots, use a box with a low entry, and put beds somewhere warm and easy to reach
- Some seniors develop **cognitive dysfunction**: loud night-time crying, getting lost, a reversed sleep cycle. Tell your vet; there are ways to help

## 🚨 Emergencies

Take your cat to a vet **right away**, without waiting, if you see:

- **A male cat straining in the litter box with little or no urine**: possibly a urinary blockage, which can be fatal within hours
- **Open-mouth breathing, fast or labored breathing**, pale or bluish gums or tongue
- **No food at all for more than 24 hours** (especially risky for overweight cats, who can develop fatty liver disease)
- Repeated vomiting or diarrhea, or blood in either
- Possible poisoning (lilies, human medicine, chocolate, insecticides, essential oils…) or swallowed thread or string
- Seizures, wobbling, sudden inability to stand, or **back legs suddenly limp and cold** (possibly a blood clot)
- A fall from height, a car accident or an animal bite, **even if they seem fine**
- Heatstroke, electric shock or burns

**On the way to the vet**

- Stay calm, wrap your cat in a towel and put them in the carrier. Injured cats may bite, so protect yourself
- **Don't try to make them vomit** and don't give human medicine. If they ate something, bring the packaging
- If thread or string is sticking out of the mouth or rear, **don't pull it**

> 🚨 Save the address and number of your nearest **24-hour emergency vet** now. In a real emergency it saves precious time.

## 🧰 First-aid kit and budgeting

**Keep at home**

- Phone numbers and addresses of your vet and the 24-hour emergency vet
- Medical and vaccination records (a photo on your phone works)
- Carrier, a large towel, disposable pads
- Gauze, bandages, pet saline and wound cleanser
- A pet digital thermometer (a cat's normal temperature is about 38–39 °C / 100.4–102.2 °F)

**Know the costs**

- Monthly: food, litter and parasite control are fairly predictable
- Yearly: vaccines and check-ups, plus blood tests for seniors
- **The unexpected**: emergencies and surgery can cost a lot. Put a little into an **emergency fund** each month, or look into what pet insurance would cover

## 🌈 Saying goodbye

- As your cat ages, keep an eye on **quality of life** together with your vet: can they eat and drink, use the box on their own, are they in ongoing pain, do they still want to be with you?
- In late-stage illness, **pain relief and comfort care** matter just as much: a soft bed, water and litter within easy reach, a quiet space
- When suffering can't be relieved, euthanasia is a way to spare them more pain. It's a hard decision; talk openly with your vet. You don't have to carry it alone
- Grief for a cat is real. Give yourself time, talk to people who understand, and consider keeping photos or writing down your memories together
