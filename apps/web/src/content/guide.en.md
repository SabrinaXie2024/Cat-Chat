# Cat Care Guide

From the day your cat comes home to their senior years, here's what matters most. Every cat is different, so for anything about health or medication, follow your vet's advice.

## 🧺 Before your cat comes home

**Essentials**

- **Litter box and litter**: ideally about 1.5× your cat's body length. Try a small bag first (tofu, clay or mixed) to see what your cat likes
- **Food**: start with whatever the shelter or breeder was feeding, then switch gradually
- **Food and water bowls**: ceramic or stainless steel are easiest to keep clean. Keep the water bowl apart from the food
- **Scratching posts**: at least one vertical and one horizontal
- **Carrier**: for vet visits and moving; left out at home it also makes a cozy den
- **Wand toys and small toys**
- **Brush and nail clippers**

**Make your home safe**

- Secure **windows and balconies**. Falls from height are common and insect screens won't hold a cat; install proper netting
- Put away **cords, hair ties, string, plastic bags and sewing thread**. Swallowed string-like objects are very dangerous
- Keep toilet lids and washer/dryer doors closed
- Check your plants: **lilies are extremely toxic to cats**; even the pollen or vase water can be fatal

> ⚠️ Licking a little lily pollen (true lilies and daylilies) can cause acute kidney failure in cats. It's safest to keep lilies out of a cat home entirely.

## 🏠 The first week

- **Start with one quiet room** with a litter box, water, food and somewhere to hide. Let your cat come out to explore on their own terms; don't force cuddles
- Hiding and eating little for a day or two is normal. But if your cat **eats nothing at all for more than 24 hours**, call your vet
- If you have other cats, **keep the newcomer separate for 1–2 weeks** until a vet check is clear, then introduce them slowly: swap scents first, then let them see each other through a door or gate
- **See a vet within the first week** to check for parasites, ear mites and infectious disease, and plan vaccines and deworming

> 💡 A slow blink is how cats say they feel safe and friendly. Try slowly blinking at your cat first; they'll often blink back.

## 🍽️ Food and feeding

**Choosing food**

- Cats are **carnivores**: pick food labelled "complete and balanced" with meat listed first
- Kitten food until about 12 months, then adult food; senior formulas are an option for older cats
- **Wet food helps a lot with hydration**. Many cats don't drink much, so mixing wet and dry works well

**How much**

- Start from the amount on the package and adjust for your cat's weight and body shape. Ideal: you can feel the ribs but not see them
- For adults, 2–3 measured meals a day keeps weight in check better than a bowl that's always full
- Change food slowly, **over 7–10 days**, mixing in more of the new food each day. Sudden switches often cause diarrhea

**Water**

- Refresh water daily. Several bowls around the home, or a fountain, encourage drinking
- Keep water away from the litter box

**Never feed your cat**

- Onion, garlic, leeks and chives (raw or cooked; they damage red blood cells)
- Chocolate, coffee, tea or anything with caffeine
- Grapes and raisins
- Alcohol, raw dough
- Anything containing xylitol
- Cooked bones, including fish and chicken bones (they splinter)
- Milk: most adult cats are lactose intolerant and get diarrhea

> ⚠️ Never give your cat human medicine. Acetaminophen (paracetamol, found in many cold and fever remedies) is extremely dangerous to cats, even in tiny doses.

## 🚽 Litter boxes

- The rule of thumb is **one box per cat, plus one**: two cats, three boxes
- Put them somewhere **quiet and easy to reach**, away from food and water and from noisy appliances like washing machines
- **Scoop at least once a day**, replace all the litter every 1–2 weeks and wash the box. Avoid strongly scented cleaners
- Watch what's in the box: urine clumps suddenly more or smaller, very loose stool or blood all signal a health problem

> 💡 A cat that stops using the litter box isn't taking revenge. Usually the box isn't clean enough or is in the wrong place, or your cat is unwell (urinary problems are common). Get a vet check first.

## 🩺 Health and vet care

**Vaccines**

- **Core vaccine** (feline panleukopenia, herpesvirus and calicivirus; often called FVRCP): kittens usually start at **8–9 weeks**, with a dose every 3–4 weeks until **16 weeks or older**, a booster a year later, then every 1–3 years
- **Rabies**: as required locally and advised by your vet
- Your vet will set the exact schedule. Keep an eye on your cat for a day after each shot

**Parasite control**

- **Internal (worms)**: typically every 3 months; kittens more often, as your vet advises
- **External (fleas, ticks)**: typically monthly. Indoor-only cats can still get fleas carried in on people
- Use **cat-specific** products only. Dog flea treatments often contain ingredients (such as permethrin) that are toxic to cats

**Spaying / neutering**

- Usually recommended **around 5–6 months**; agree the timing with your vet
- It reduces yowling and spraying during heat and lowers the risk of uterine infection and mammary tumors
- Neutered cats gain weight more easily, so watch portions

**Regular check-ups**

- Adults: **once a year**. Seniors: **every six months**
- Cats are experts at hiding pain; regular check-ups catch problems earlier

## ✨ Everyday care

- **Brushing**: short-haired cats 1–2 times a week, long-haired cats ideally daily. Less shedding, fewer hairballs
- **Nail trims**: every 2–3 weeks. Only trim the clear tip; **avoid the pink quick**
- **Teeth**: use a cat toothbrush and cat toothpaste, **never human toothpaste**. Tartar and gum disease are common in adult cats
- **Baths**: most cats keep themselves clean and rarely need one. Bathe only when very dirty or advised by a vet, and dry them fully afterwards
- **Eyes and ears**: wipe away eye discharge with a damp cotton pad. Lots of dark ear discharge may mean ear mites; see a vet

## 🐾 Behavior and play

**Play every day**

- At least **twice a day for 10–15 minutes**. Use a wand toy to mimic the hunt (stalk, chase, pounce, catch) and let your cat "catch" the prey at the end so they feel satisfied
- **Don't play with your bare hands**; cats learn hands are prey and bite more as adults
- Give them places to climb and hide, like cat trees and cardboard boxes

**Reading body language**

- **Tail straight up, tip curved**: happy, saying hello
- **Tail lashing side to side**: irritated and about to get angry; stop what you're doing
- **Ears flat back, pupils wide, body low**: scared or ready to defend
- **Belly up**: a sign of trust, not necessarily an invitation to rub it
- **Purring**: usually relaxed and content, but cats also purr when in pain or stressed

> 💡 Punishment and shouting only make a cat afraid of you. If your cat does something you don't want, like scratching the sofa, offer an alternative (a scratching post right next to it) and reward them when they use it.

## 📅 Life stages

**Kittens (0–12 months)**

- Full of energy: lots of play and socialization with different people, sounds and places
- Keep up with vaccines and deworming; feed kitten food

**Adults (1–7 years)**

- Regular meals and exercise; keep weight in check
- Yearly check-ups, parasite control and boosters

**Seniors (7+, with 11+ considered geriatric)**

- Switch to a senior formula and watch their weight
- Check-ups every six months; look out for **drinking and peeing more** (can point to kidney disease, diabetes or hyperthyroidism)
- Joints may stiffen: add steps up to favorite high spots and use a box with a lower entry

## 🚨 Emergencies

Take your cat to a vet **right away**, without waiting, if you see:

- **A male cat straining in the litter box with little or no urine**: possibly a urinary blockage, which can be fatal within hours
- **Open-mouth breathing, fast or labored breathing**
- **No food at all for more than 24 hours** (especially risky for overweight cats, who can develop fatty liver disease)
- Repeated vomiting or diarrhea, or blood in either
- Possible poisoning (lilies, human medicine, chocolate, insecticides…)
- Seizures, wobbling, or suddenly being unable to stand
- A fall from height, a car accident or an animal bite, even if they seem fine

> 🚨 Save the address and number of your nearest **24-hour emergency vet** now. In a real emergency it saves precious time.
